Police & Criminal Justice (警察・刑事司法)

Japan feels extraordinarily safe on the street, and the police are highly visible. The criminal justice system behind that calm is not a mirror of Western adversarial courts: prosecutors gate charges, confessions weigh heavily, and the death penalty still exists. Visitors rarely touch this system; understanding it explains news headlines and why legal advice in Japan matters if you are ever detained.

On the street: koban, patrol, and the white baton

Koban (交番): Small neighborhood police boxes — map pins every few blocks in cities. Officers give directions, take lost-property reports, and are the face of the National Police Agency (NPA).

Community policing: Bicycle patrols and foot beats are normal. Officers carry a keibo (警棒) — the straight white baton Westerners often notice (not a “martial arts stick,” but a standard issue control tool, with training in joint locks and strikes in police curriculum).

Emergency: 110 for police, 119 for fire/ambulance. Non-Japanese speakers: say “English please” slowly; major cities have some bilingual staff, not guaranteed.

Foreign residents: You must carry ID (residence card or passport). Random ID checks are legal; stay calm, ask for a badge number if unsure, and request embassy contact if arrested.

How a case moves: police → prosecutor → court

Police investigate and send a case to the public prosecutor (検察, kensatsu).

Western misunderstanding #1: Japan’s famous “99% conviction rate” applies after indictment — not to everyone arrested. Prosecutors drop or suspend many cases. They only indict when they believe a trial will end in conviction with the evidence in hand (often summarized abroad as a very high internal certainty bar — “we don’t charge unless it’s essentially proven”). That makes trials look one-sided: the state rarely brings weak cases to court.

Western misunderstanding #2: Low crime ≠ light process. Detention can be long before trial; bail exists but is granted less often than in the US or UK for serious cases.

Defense lawyers: You have the right to counsel, but interrogation culture still centers on getting a statement early. Do not assume “I’ll explain and they’ll let me go” without a lawyer if anything serious is alleged.

Confessions and interrogation (自白)

Historically, Japanese criminal justice leaned on confessions (自白, jihaku) as the “king of evidence.” Police may detain suspects for up to 23 days per charge (with extensions via rearrest tactics in controversial cases), often in a small room with long prosecutor-led questioning.

“Hook or by crook” history: Documented coerced confessions, sleep deprivation, and intimidation drove famous wrongful convictions (see below). Media and NGOs campaigned for decades on yami shōgen (闇証言, shady testimony) and fabricated records.

Reforms: Partial recording of interrogations for serious cases began expanding after 2016–2019 reforms, but full transparency is still debated. The culture of “regret and apologize” (悔悟) remains socially powerful even outside crime.

Practical note: If detained, sign nothing you do not fully understand; insist on a lawyer and embassy. Interpreters are required in theory — quality varies.

Death penalty (死刑)

Method: Hanging — still the sole legal method. Executions are carried out in secret; family and lawyers are notified only after the fact.

Who gets it: Not routine. Reserved for aggravated murder — multiple killings, extreme cruelty, robbery-murder, etc. Mass murder and terrorism cases have drawn death sentences in recent years.

Public opinion: Polls consistently show majority support for keeping the death penalty, unlike much of Europe. Debate exists but abolition is not politically near.

Death row conditions: Inmates live under strict isolation for years or decades — single cells, limited contact, exercise often alone. The wait itself is part of the punishment; executions can come with very short notice (same-day morning notification is reported practice).

Wrongful convictions and late exoneration

The same confession-centric system produced reversals that shocked the public:

Hakamada Iwao: Former boxer; death row for decades over a 1966 quadruple murder; released and retried amid evidence of tampered clothing; a symbol of “retrial hell” (再審の壁).

Other retrials: 1980s–2000s “serial false charge” scandals (e.g. Fukawa, Toyo case echoes) pushed some reforms. Recent years saw acquittals and retrial orders after 30–40+ years in a few cases, often when DNA or new scrutiny undermined old confessions.

Lesson: High trust in police/prosecutors + low acquittal rates do not mean zero errors — they can mean errors are discovered very late.

Baseline crime: Japan Times vs a large US city

Violent street crime in Japan is so low that English readers notice the Japan Times police blotter: some days the “worst” reported incident is a clothesline thief (下着盗み) — laundry stolen from a balcony — or a bicycle without a lock, petty shoplifting, or a drunk argument with no injury.

Compare a large US city: Same day’s metro pages might carry shootings, carjackings, transit assaults, and homicide counts — different planet, not just different policy. Tokyo is not crime-free (see fraud, chikan, upskirting, occasional gang flashes), but the floor of daily violence is wildly lower than Chicago, Philadelphia, Houston, etc.

Do not confuse low violence with soft law: Japan still prosecutes hard when you cross the line — drugs, knives, groping, fraud. The surprise is how rare gun homicide is, not how forgiving the system is.

Media bias: When something violent does happen, it becomes national news for days — because it is unusual.

Visitors: what actually matters to you

Drugs: Zero-tolerance reputation is real — small cannabis amounts can mean detention and deportation.

Knives: Strict rules on blade length; carrying without reason is an offense.

Traffic: Cycling drunk, serious accidents, and hit-and-run are criminal, not just civil.

Disputes: Touching, grabbing, or prolonged confrontation (including on trains) can become chikan (groping) or assault allegations — do not “explain your way out” without legal help if police are called.
