Shopping & Vending (買い物・自動販売機)

Japan is a retail laboratory: more vending machines per capita than almost anywhere, konbini on every corner, department-store ritual, covered shotengai streets, and whole neighborhoods that sell one kind of thing (books, electronics, otaku goods). See also Konbini culture for 24/7 food and services.

Vending machines (自動販売機, jidōhanbaiki) — a true specialty

Ubiquity: Roughly millions nationwide — sidewalks, train platforms, mountain trails, rural bus stops. Hot coffee in winter, cold barley tea in summer, same machine.

Beyond drinks: Ice cream, hot snacks, bananas, fresh eggs, ramen bowls, umbrellas, SIM cards, batteries, face masks — seasonal and regional surprises (Hokkaido corn soup, shrine omamori vending near temples).

Why it works: Low vandalism, coinless/mobile pay, restocking routes, trust. Tourist photos are cliché because the density is real.

Vs konbini: Machines for instant single items; konbini for meals, ATMs, tickets, toilets. You will use both daily.

Department stores (デパート) & depachika (デパ地下)

Names: Mitsukoshi, Isetan, Takashimaya, Daimaru — flagship buildings in Shinjuku, Ginza, Osaka Umeda.

Basement food halls: Depachika — premium bento, wagashi, foreign cheese, free samples on weekends. Gift culture (omiyage) runs through here; packaging is half the product.

Etiquette: Polite staff, bagging ritual, tax-free counters for tourists on floors marked duty-free.

Shotengai (商店街) — covered shopping streets

Local arcade roofs over mom-and-pop shops: butcher, greengrocer, 100-yen chain, barber, tiny ramen. Survive suburban Japan where malls failed; some are sleepy, others bounce back with youth markets.

Try: Yanaka Ginza (Tokyo old-town vibe), Kawagoe Kurazukuri, neighborhood arcs near your Airbnb — not every shotengai is photogenic, but they are how residents actually shop.

Don Quijote (ドン・キホーテ, “Donki”)

Discount chaos in the best way: multi-floor maze, tax-free counters, snacks, cosmetics, costumes, luggage, electronics. Mega stores in Shibuya, Dotonbori, tourist districts — open late, loud jingle, packed aisles.

Tourist use: One-stop omiyage and weird Kit-Kat flavors; not always cheapest on every SKU, but unforgettable.

Book Off & second-hand chains

Book Off (ブックオフ): Used books, manga, games, CDs, DVDs — graded condition, buyback culture.

Hard Off / Hobby Off: Cameras, guitars, audio gear, model kits — treasure hunting for collectors.

Why it matters: Japan respects resale; you can build a library or game collection cheap if you read Japanese (or hunt art books).

Music stores — still lively

Streaming did not kill physical fandom here. Tower Records (Shibuya flagship still iconic), Disk Union (jazz, metal, vinyl caves), Tower/Village Vanguard lifestyle books + music.

Recofan / HMV: Used and new; event corners for idol handshake tickets and promo displays.

Experience: Whole floors by genre; listening booths; obi strips on CDs as collectible art.

Specialist districts — one shop type, one neighborhood

Jimbocho (神保町, Tokyo): Used and new bookstores — academic, manga, maps — dozens along one grid.

Akihabara (秋葉原): Electronics, PC parts, retro games, maid cafes, anime goods — otaku capital (calmer weekdays).

Nakano Broadway / Ikebukuro: Mandarake and figure shops — collector otaku depth.

Nipponbashi (Osaka): Kansai’s Akihabara — Den Den Town.

Kappabashi (Tokyo): Restaurant supply street — fake food samples, knives, ceramics.

Pattern: Cities cluster commerce by type; wander one district per obsession instead of mall genericism.

100-yen shops & drugstores

Daiso, Seria, Can Do: Quality housewares at ¥100 (plus tax) — travel adapters, chopsticks, souvenirs.

Matsumoto Kiyoshi, Sundrug: Cosmetics, medicine, snacks — tax-free shelves for tourists; konbini cannot match breadth.
