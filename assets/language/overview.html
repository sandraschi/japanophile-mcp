Japanese: overview and basics

Japanese (Nihongo) is the national language of Japan, spoken by roughly 125 million people as a first language. For learners coming from English or European languages, it is often classified as "hard" not because the spoken grammar is impossibly exotic, but because literacy, honorific register, and pitch accent add layers that do not exist in English at the same scale. Treat spoken competence and literacy as partially separate projects: you can hold travel conversations while still learning kanji, and you can read manga with furigana while your keigo is still rough.

Language family and history

Japanese is traditionally treated as a language isolate: it is not demonstrably related to Korean, Chinese, or Altaic languages in the way French is related to Spanish. Written Japanese absorbed massive lexical and orthographic influence from Classical Chinese over centuries (kanji, compounds, Sino-Japanese readings), but the core grammar (SOV order, particles, agglutinative verb morphology) remains distinct. Modern standard Japanese (hyojungo) is based on the Tokyo dialect and is what broadcasters, textbooks, and standardized exams assume.

What "basic Japanese" actually means

At a functional A1–A2 level you should be able to: introduce yourself; use numbers, time, and dates; order food; ask directions; handle konbini and train-station routines; and recognize the most frequent polite patterns (desu/masu, te-form chains for requests). That corresponds roughly to the easiest standardized exam band (N5) and the first third of most beginner textbooks (Genki I, Minna no Nihongo I, Marugoto A1–A2). See the Exams & benchmarks tab for what N5–N1 mean.

At B1 (roughly N4–N3 band) you add: longer paragraphs; reason and result (node/kara); ability and experience (koto ga aru); passive and causative in simple uses; and enough kanji to read labeled public life (signs, menus, simple news headlines with furigana). Fluency is not speed-reading literature yet; it is stable comprehension of daily life.

Core structural features (preview)

  • SOV word order: the verb typically ends the clause. Adjectives and nouns take linking morphemes before the verb.
  • Particles (joshi): short function words (wa, ga, o, ni, de, to, kara, made, mo) mark roles. Many errors at intermediate level are particle choice, not vocabulary.
  • Verbs and adjectives inflect for tense, polarity, and politeness; nouns do not inflect for number or gender.
  • Topics and comments: wa often marks what the sentence is "about"; ga often marks new or focused subjects (not interchangeable with English subject).
  • Register: plain vs polite (da/desu, dictionary vs masu-stem) is only the start; keigo (honorific/humble) appears constantly in service, business, and healthcare.

Three scripts (why literacy feels heavy)

Modern Japanese uses hiragana (native grammar and words), katakana (loanwords and emphasis), and kanji (meaning-bearing characters, often several readings each). A single sentence may mix all three. Children learn hiragana first, then katakana, then kanji year by year through school. Adult learners usually parallel-track spoken course + kanji deck + reading with furigana.

Dialects and variation

Every learner meets standard Japanese first. Regional dialects (Kansai-ben, Tohoku, Okinawan languages and Ryukyuan continuum) differ in pitch, endings, and lexicon. Media often stereotypes dialect for comedy; living in Osaka or Fukuoka will recalibrate your ear. For exams and business, stay with hyojungo until you are solid, then explore dialect as culture—not as test Japanese.

Sensible first-year roadmap

  1. Master hiragana and katakana (reading and writing, not just recognition).
  2. Work through one structured textbook with audio; shadow short dialogs daily.
  3. Start kanji with meaning + readings for the most frequent 300–500 characters; do not wait until "later."
  4. Build a sentence-mining habit: every new word enters with a full sentence, not an isolated flashcard when possible.
  5. Take a mock exam at N5/N4 level after ~6–9 months to calibrate weak areas (listening often lags reading for self-studiers).
