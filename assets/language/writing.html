Writing systems: kana and kanji

Japanese orthography is a stack of historical layers. Learners who skip kana and jump to romaji-only apps usually plateau early because real life in Japan is not romanized. The goal of this tab is a clear mental model, not memorizing every rule on day one.

Hiragana (ひらがな)

Forty-six basic mora plus voiced/semi-voiced variants and small ya/yu/yo combinations. Hiragana spells native Japanese words, grammatical endings, and particles. Every verb ending you learn in Genki appears in hiragana. Learn to write from memory, not only to read app fonts—stroke direction matters for later kanji.

Common pitfalls: confusing は (particle wa) with わ; を (object marker o) with お; long vowels spelled as two characters (おう, うう, えい) that are one mora in speech.

Katakana (カタカナ)

Same mora inventory, different shapes. Used for gairaigo (loanwords), onomatopoeia, scientific names, and emphasis (like italic caps in English). Loanwords are often reshaped to Japanese phonology: com-pu-u-ta-, re-su-to-ra-n. Listening practice must include katakana-heavy domains (coffee orders, tech, fashion).

Kanji (漢字)

Logographic characters borrowed from Chinese, adapted over centuries. Each kanji may have:

  • On'yomi — Sino-Japanese readings, common in compounds (e.g. 学校 gakkou).
  • Kun'yomi — native readings, common in verbs/adjectives or standalone nouns (e.g. 食べる taberu).
  • Okurigana — trailing hiragana showing inflection (食べます).

Joyo kanji (2,136 characters) define newspaper and official literacy targets. Exam kanji lists (N5–N1) are a subset curve for foreigners, not identical to school grades. Frequency lists (e.g. top 2,500 by corpus) often beat grade order for adult self-study.

Orthography in the wild

Vertical text still appears in novels, some signage, and historical displays; web and UI are mostly horizontal. Ruby (furigana) small kana above kanji helps learners and disambiguates rare readings. Punctuation uses full-width comma and period (、。); quotation marks differ from English.

Romaji and romanization systems

Romaji is a training wheel for IME setup and week-one survival, not a reading medium. English-language learners still collide with two different romanization policies:

Modified Hepburn (what almost everyone actually uses) — shi, chi, tsu, fu, ji; long vowels marked with macron (ō) or doubled vowels (oo, uu) in careful typography. This is what you see on Japanese passports, road signs aimed at foreigners, most textbooks (Genki, Minna), Jisho.org, Wikipedia, and airline tickets. If a phrasebook says Tokyo, Shinjuku, sushi, it is Hepburn-shaped.

Kunrei-shiki (訓令式, the "official" Japanese government standard) — taught in Japanese primary-school romanization drills and some domestic typography. Same consonants mapped differently: si not shi, ti not chi, tu not tsu, hu not fu, zi not ji, sya not sha. The state promotes it for consistency with kana rows; internationally it failed to displace Hepburn — learners hit old worksheets or NHK materials and wonder why しろ reads siro in one book and shiro in another. Nihon-shiki (ISO-style) is a close cousin with similar friction.

Practical rule: learn kana quickly, treat Hepburn as the English-default romanization, recognize Kunrei when it appears on Japanese-produced charts, and never argue about Osaka vs Ôsaka on the internet when you could be learning 大阪.

Kanji learning approaches (and what replaced them)

There is no single sacred order — only tradeoffs between meaning, readings, writing, and retention.

  • Heisig, Remembering the Kanji — Invented English-story mnemonics per character; stroke order; readings deliberately deferred. Strength: fast visual recognition for adults. Weakness: second pass needed for Japanese readings; stories do not match native etymology. Still a cult classic on the shelf.
  • Kodansha Kanji Learner's Course (KKLC) — Meaning + readings + compounds in one graded sequence; closer to "one pass" literacy.
  • Japanese school grade order — Matches children in Japan; good if you raise bilingual kids, slower for adult spoken-first paths.
  • Frequency / exam band lists — Top-N corpus order or N5→N1 kanji sets; efficient for test dates, weak on joyful reading.
  • Component / radical first — Learn 部首 and recurring phonetic components; powers long-term guessing of compounds (see Practice kanji wall).

Apps largely replaced paper mnemonics for new learners: WaniKani (SRS + stories + readings + vocab lockstep), Kanshudo, Kanji Study, Bunpro kanji add-ons, and Anki decks cloned from RTK or KKLC. They automate scheduling and audio; they do not remove the need to read real sentences. Many people who bought Heisig in 2005 now maintain WaniKani streaks instead — same idea (chunked mnemonics), different delivery. Paper still wins for annotation and kissaten study without battery drain; apps win for daily review hygiene.

Study sequence that works

  1. Two weeks: fluent hiragana + katakana (read + write).
  2. Parallel: 5–10 new kanji daily with meaning + one common compound each.
  3. Start graded readers or manga with furigana early; do not wait for "enough" kanji.
  4. Use the Practice kanji wall and stroke-order game for spacing and radical intuition.
