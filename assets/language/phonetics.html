Phonetics, mora, and pitch accent

Japanese is not tonal like Mandarin (syllable-fixed tones). It uses mora (timing units) and pitch accent (high-low patterns within words). Bad pitch rarely blocks comprehension for learners, but native speakers notice flat foreign accent immediately.

Mora inventory

Each mora is roughly one beat. Long vowels are two mora (おばあさん obaasan). Geminate consonants (small っ) hold one mora of silence before the stop (がっこう gakkou). Vowel devoicing (museika 無声化) is covered below — it is not optional detail; it changes what "correct" sounds like in the wild.

Five vowels

/a i u e o/ are fairly stable, pure vowels. If you already speak Spanish or German, the mapping is mostly one-to-one: your /a/, /i/, /u/, /e/, /o/ are close to Japanese without the extra glides English adds. That is why continental European learners often sound cleaner on vowels early.

Native English speakers carry a different habit: English vowels are often diphthongs or centralized (English "gate" drags toward /ei/; Japanese ゲート stays one clean /e/ mora). Common beginner errors: turning お into "oh-w", え into "ay", う into strong English "oo", or inserting schwa where Japanese has none. Drill minimal pairs in kana (あ/お, い/え, す/せ) and compare to a Spanish or German recording, not to English spelling.

Stress, rhythm, and pitch (do not import English stress)

English hammers lexical stress: one syllable loud and long, others reduced ("JApanese" vs "japaNESE"). Japanese does not work that way. Mora timing is relatively even; meaning is carried by pitch accent patterns, not by crushing one syllable the English way. English speakers who "stress" the wrong mora often sound fine to themselves but foreign instantly — flat where a drop is expected, or a fake loud peak where Tokyo Japanese stays level (heiban).

Rule of thumb: stop trying to make Japanese "bounce" like English. Listen for after-the-drop patterns on nouns, then particles; shadow short clips rather than chanting romaji with English stress marks.

Vowel devoicing (無声化) — the odd rules

High vowels /i/ and /u/ are often devoiced (whispered, almost silent) when they sit between voiceless consonants or in common grammatical endings spoken quickly. This is systematic in standard speech but surprises learners who were taught to "pronounce every vowel clearly."

  • です / ます — frequently sound like des' / mas' (barely audible u).
  • すし, つき, くつ — the u or i can nearly disappear between /s/, /t/, /k/, /h/, /p/ environments.
  • Gender and style — devoicing is more aggressive in fast, crisp speech; some speakers and dialects keep vowels fuller. Both exist; textbooks understate the devoiced norm.

Why it feels "strange": the spelling (kana) still shows full vowels, dictionaries may show full-vowel citation forms, and devoicing is not the same as deleting a mora — timing often still reserves a beat. Learners who hyper-articulate every u sound textbook-correct but unnatural; learners who drop vowels randomly sound sloppy. Train on recordings of full sentences, not isolated words shouted slowly.

Consonants worth drilling separately below; devoicing interacts with them (especially /s/, /sh/, /t/, /k/, /p/ clusters).

Consonants worth drilling

  • Fu — bilabial fricative, not English f or h.
  • R — flap, between l and r.
  • Tsu — single affricate, not t + su.
  • N — moraic ん assimilates (before labials → m sound, etc.).

Pitch accent patterns

Standard Japanese words have one accent drop (some are heiban — flat after particle attachment rules). Example famous pair: hashi (bridge vs chopsticks) differs by pattern, not vowels. Dictionaries mark Tokyo accent; regional varieties differ (Kansai patterns are not identical).

Study tactics: shadow NHK news briefs; use pitch-aware apps; record yourself; do not rely on romaji stress marks (English stress ≠ Japanese pitch). See above: even rhythm and devoiced vowels matter as much as accent diagrams.

Listening vs speaking

Listening lags when study is reading-heavy. Schedule daily audio even on kanji-heavy weeks: textbook CDs, podcast for learners, drama clips with replay. Standardized exam listening sections punish inference and fast dialogue, not only vocabulary.

Phonetic writing

Learners may see IPA in linguistics texts; textbooks use kana. Avoid romaji-only listening drills after month one.
