Yakuza / Boryokudan (ヤクザ・暴力団)

There is no romantic “yakuza tab” in travel brochures — only reality, fiction, and law. Boryokudan is the legal term for designated organized crime groups. Most visitors never meet them; understanding the topic explains pachinko politics, construction bids, tattoos in onsen rules, and why Japan cracked down on gang offices. Related: Games & gambling, Police & justice.

Origins: bakuto, tekiya, and the modern yakuza

Bakuto (博徒): Gamblers — one root of yakuza lore (see samurai-era gambling).

Tekiya (的屋): Street merchants and festival stall operators — another root; protection rackets at shrines and fairs.

Name “yakuza”: From a losing hand in a traditional card game (8-9-3 = useless) — self-deprecating gang slang turned badge.

Post-war: Black markets, amphetamines, dock labor; then real estate, finance, entertainment. Peak visibility in the 1980s–90s bubble era.

Major groups (simplified)

Yamaguchi-gumi (山口組): Largest syndicate, based in Kobe; split factions in the 2010s weakened central control but headlines remain.

Sumiyoshi-kai, Inagawa-kai: Other nationwide federations with different internal cultures.

Structure: oyabun-kobun (boss–underboss) pseudo-family; rituals, sake cups, crests — copied endlessly in movies.

Gaijin image: Full irezumi back pieces, missing pinkies (yubitsume apology cuts), sharp suits — partly true, partly Showa nostalgia; many gangs now discourage flashy tattoos to avoid police heat.

Money: gambling, loans, and gray business

Pachinko & parlors: Historically used for laundering and skim; chains corporatized but ties linger in scandals.

Traditional loansharking: Sarakin (サラ金) and street gai-shakkin (街金) at illegal rates; collectors who “visit daily” until you pay — the Kaiji / yakuza-film template. Yubitsume and office intimidation still appear in cases, but much lending moved online or through shell companies.

Construction & bids: “Bid-rigging” (dango) and dispatch labor on public works — yakuza as enforcers and brokers.

Crime today: ore-ore, flash crews, and internet scams

Modern Japan’s headline crime is less tattooed backs in alleys than phones and accounts. Gangs, semi-legit fraud shops, and purely foreign call centers overlap; police treat victims the same regardless. Remember the baseline: on many days the Japan Times police round-up tops out at laundry theft or petty nuisance — unthinkable as “lead crime” in a large US city (see Police & justice).

Ore-ore scams (オレオレ詐欺, “It’s me! It’s me!”): Call or message pretending to be a son/grandson in trouble — “wrong number but trust me,” urgent transfer to a “lawyer” or “police account.” Targets elderly parents; billions of yen lost yearly at peak. Call scripts sold in manuals; gang-linked “receiver” bank accounts (受け子).

Loansharking 2.0: SNS and apps recruit debtors; harassment via LINE; personal info leaks; still the same trap as Showa street lenders, faster distribution.

Flash gangs (スマートフォンで募集): Short-notice robberies (luxury bags, watches, smash-and-grab) coordinated on Telegram, X, or closed groups — often recruiting young part-timers (“稼げるバイト”) who may not know it is yakuza-backed. 2020s Tokyo/Kansai cases mixed Japanese minors and foreign nationals; not classic oyabun hierarchy but same money wash.

Other internet-facilitated fraud: - Romance / pig-butchering: Long fake relationships steering victims to fake investment sites. - Billing fraud (請求書詐欺): Fake invoices to companies; weak accounting clicks “pay.” - Refund / overpayment scams: “We sent too much — wire back.” - Illegal online casinos & crypto: Offshore sites, money mules, laundering through pachinko-style loops. - Sextortion: Dating-app blackmail — rising among students and salarymen.

Chikan (痴漢) & upskirting — not yakuza, but national crime talk: Organized gangs did not invent these; they are the everyday sexual offenses Japan still wrestles with on packed trains and streets. Chikan — groping in crowded rail cars; women-only cars, station posters, and sting arrests are routine in Tokyo/Osaka. Foreigners who grab or stare-down accusers can face real jail time — no “drunk tourist” pass. Upskirting / hidden camera (盗撮, “upskirt” videos): Often called one of the stupidest crimes — high risk (cameras everywhere, vigilant commuters), low payoff, yet persistent; nationwide crackdowns and 2019+ stronger laws treat sneaker shots and bathroom films as serious sex crimes, not pranks. Phone storage and resale threads tie some cases to porn sites; penalties can exceed what offenders expect.

Tourist angle: You are unlikely to get ore-ore calls in Japanese, but ATM fraud, bag snatches, and nightclub overcharging happen — see Police & justice. Do not accept “easy job” DMs recruiting for theft.

Law and decline narrative

Anti-Organized Crime Act (1991+): Prefectures designate boryokudan; banks and landlords can refuse gang transactions.

2010s–20s crackdown: Office raids, executive arrests, membership falling in official statistics — partly success, partly gangs going underground or foreign.

Foreign gangs: Chinese/Korean networks, cybercrime — police attention shifting; classic “yakuza movie” era is aging.

Tourists: Do not seek “yakuza tours” or illegal hosts; tattoo bans in onsen are about norms, not personal judgment — see daily life pages.

Violence: movies, postwar guns, and what residents actually see

Film vs street: Yakuza movies (Battles Without Honor and Humanity, Outrage, Like a Dragon) are often shockingly brutal — sword work, mass beatings, ritual murder. That tone sells tickets; it has very little relation to everyday reality for ordinary people in modern Japan.

Postwar was worse: Through the 1940s–60s (and bubble-era turf wars), real violence spiked — many guns sloshing around, street shootings, and open gang warfare made headlines. Strict gun control and boryokudan crackdowns since then changed the surface: today’s groups lean on fraud, loans, and white-collar coercion more than daily public gunplay.

Rarely seen: Even long-term residents can go years without witnessing open gang violence. When it flares in public, it is often nightlife — e.g. a large, visibly Western patron, drunk and out of line in a club, then several compact enforcers (often much smaller on paper) beating him and leaving fast — sometimes including a car in the street. Spectacular for bystanders, still uncommon. The size mismatch is part of the shock; it is not a cartoon “weak Japan” trope — trained numbers, alcohol, and venue rules beat height. Japan’s low gun rate means fists, boots, and vehicles, not the shootout reflex familiar from the US; that is still serious assault, not “safe because no bullets.”

Do not romanticize: One dramatic night does not make gangs chivalrous; victims and bystanders still get hurt, and police treat assault as assault.

Fiction vs fact

Games & film: Yakuza (Like a Dragon), Battles Without Honor and Humanity, Outrage — stylized honor codes and extreme violence on screen (see section above).

Reality: Predatory lending, human trafficking cases, meth trade — not chivalry. Victims include Japanese citizens and vulnerable migrants.

Respect: Treat gang iconography as serious, not cosplay for Instagram in Japan.
